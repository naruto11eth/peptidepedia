## What it is

Spermidine is a polyamine, found in all living cells and in reasonable quantities in food: wheat germ, aged cheese, natto, mushrooms. It is one of the more credible autophagy inducers known, acting partly by inhibiting the acetyltransferase EP300, and autophagy is among the better supported mechanisms in ageing biology.

Cellular spermidine declines with age. In model organisms, supplementing it extends lifespan, and it does so in yeast, flies, worms and mice.

## The epidemiology is genuinely interesting

Dietary spermidine intake has been associated with lower all-cause mortality in prospective cohorts, with effect sizes that observers have compared to a few years of life expectancy.

That is the kind of finding that should generate a trial rather than a product, and for once it did.

## The trial found nothing

SmartAge was a randomised, placebo-controlled trial of spermidine supplementation in older adults with subjective cognitive decline, published in JAMA Network Open in 2022. It ran twelve months. It measured memory and a panel of biomarkers.

Longer-term spermidine supplementation did not modify memory or the biomarkers compared with placebo.

That is a clean negative result, in the population most likely to buy the supplement, on the endpoint most often claimed for it, over a year. A separate exploratory trial in older men found that even 40 mg daily, far above the dose in commercial products, had minimal effects on circulating polyamine levels, which raises the prior question of whether oral dosing changes systemic exposure enough to matter.

## Where it still might work

The one area with a positive randomised signal is unrelated to ageing claims: a phase 2 trial found oral spermidine preserved salivary gland function after radiotherapy, which is a specific tissue-protection question with a plausible autophagy mechanism.

That is how this usually goes. A broad mechanism produces a narrow result somewhere unexpected, and the broad claim stays unsupported.

## Practical notes

- **Food is a credible route and a cheap one.** The mortality associations come from dietary intake. Wheat germ, aged cheese and natto deliver spermidine at a cost no supplement matches.
- **Standardisation varies widely.** Most products are wheat germ extracts, and the labelled spermidine content depends on an extraction nobody independently verifies.
- **Be clear which claim the negative trial refutes.** SmartAge tested cognition over a year and found nothing. It did not test lifespan, which is untestable at human timescales, so the epidemiology and the mechanism both survive. The specific promise of better memory does not.
