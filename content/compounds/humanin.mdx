## What it is

Humanin is a 24 amino acid peptide encoded in the mitochondrial genome, not the nuclear one. Along with [MOTS-c](/compounds/mots-c) it belongs to a small class of mitochondrial-derived peptides, and their existence rewrote part of the textbook account of what mitochondrial DNA does.

It was discovered in surviving neurons from an Alzheimer's brain, screened for factors that protected cells from amyloid toxicity. It is cytoprotective, anti-apoptotic, and acts partly through a cell surface receptor complex, meaning it functions as a signalling molecule rather than only inside the cell that makes it.

The biology is legitimately novel and interesting.

## The evidence and the gap

Circulating humanin declines with age. Higher levels have been reported in the offspring of centenarians, and in long-lived mouse models. It protects cells and tissues in a wide range of animal and cell studies covering neurodegeneration, cardiac ischaemia, diabetes and more.

There has never been a human trial. Not a phase 1, not a safety study, not a pharmacokinetic characterisation.

The association between humanin levels and longevity is the sort of correlation that could run either direction. Healthier mitochondria may produce more humanin, in which case the peptide is a marker of youthful function rather than a cause of it, and injecting it would be treating the readout.

## Practical notes

- **Anti-apoptotic is the mechanism and also the concern.** Keeping cells alive that would otherwise die is beneficial for neurons under stress and is exactly what a cancer cell requires. Nobody has studied that trade in a person.
- **This is the same tier as [MOTS-c](/compounds/mots-c) for the same reason.** Interesting mitochondrial biology, marketed considerably ahead of the human evidence.
- **A correlation in centenarians is not a protocol.** It is a starting point for research that has not yet been done.
